Local AI vs. cloud AI: which bot survives the company?

There are two kinds of intelligence on a modern companion robot. The first lives in the robot: a small chip recognises your face, reads a touch, runs an expression engine and keeps a memory. The second lives in a data centre: your voice is streamed to a large language model, and the answer comes back a second later.

Cloud AI is smarter. It can hold a conversation, answer a question about the weather and improvise a story. Local AI is more durable. It keeps working when the Wi-Fi drops, when the subscription lapses, and — crucially — when the company that made the robot runs out of money.

“A companion robot is a promise that something will be there tomorrow. A cloud dependency is a bet that a startup will be.”

We have watched this play out in public. Jibo’s servers shut down and the robot performed a farewell dance. Moxie’s cloud was switched off after its maker failed to raise another round. Anki went bankrupt and its robots were revived by a small acquirer. The pattern is not that cloud AI is bad; it is that cloud-only companionship is fragile.

What to ask before buying: Does the core personality work offline? Can the robot boot and play without an account? If the app disappears, does the hardware become a paperweight? Is there a local mode, and what exactly stops working in it?

Our shortlist deliberately mixes both. Eilik and Moflin are local-first and proud of it. Loona and EMO use cloud models for conversation but keep movement and expressions on-device. That is a reasonable compromise — as long as you know which parts are rented.

The subscription math nobody shows you

A robot’s sticker price is the beginning of its cost, not the end. Subscription plans are often framed as optional, but the useful parts — conversation, stories, cloud storage, new expressions — can sit behind the paywall. Run the four-year math before you fall for the launch video.

The point is not that subscriptions are evil. Cloud inference costs money, and a sustainable plan is better than a robot that dies. The point is that “$199” and “$199 plus $15 a month forever” are different products. We list every recurring fee in the product specs, and we flag the ones that are required for the core experience.

If you want a no-subscription household, start with Eilik, Moflin, Ropet or a STEM kit. If you want conversation and don’t mind a plan, Loona and EMO are the sweet spot — both include their core AI without a mandatory monthly fee at the time of writing.

Best first robots by age

3–5: soft, screen-free, no small parts

At this age the robot is a character, not a computer. Look for plush bodies, volume limits, no removable eyes and no app required. Joy for All and FurReal are the safe picks; BubblePal is a clever add-on for an existing teddy, but it needs an adult to set up the Wi-Fi.

6–9: cause and effect, with a safety net

This is the golden age for robot pets. Loona, Miko Mini and Little Live Pets give immediate feedback without a wall of text. Check the subscription before you buy Miko, and check the privacy settings before any camera-equipped bot enters a child’s room.

10–13: build something, break something

STEM kits beat companion bots here because the child can change the outcome. Sphero Bolt, mBot2 and LEGO SPIKE Essential all survive repeat assembly. Budget for a kit that can be rebuilt, not a one-off toy that loses its novelty in a week.

14+: the desk companion era

Teens and adults want personality, not a lesson. Eilik, EMO, Vector and AIBI are desk-sized, expressive and socially acceptable in a group chat. Check the noise level if the desk is shared, and check the cloud policy if the robot has a camera.

65+: companionship without a learning curve

Look for tactile response, a familiar form, no mandatory app and a support line that answers. Joy for All Companion Pup and Tombot Jennie are designed for older adults; ElliQ is the subscription-based, care-oriented option. Avoid anything that requires a smartphone to perform basic comfort behaviours.

The five-minute checklist

  • Offline test: Can it do its core trick with Wi-Fi off?
  • Subscription test: What stops working if you cancel?
  • Repair test: Are batteries and motors replaceable? Is there a service partner in your country?
  • Privacy test: Does it have a camera or microphone? Is there a physical shutter or mute?
  • Support test: Is there a human email, or only a chatbot and a Discord?

If a product page cannot answer three of those five, it is not ready for your money — no matter how good the launch video looks.
